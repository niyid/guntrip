Part Two — Cambodia, 1975–1979

The Boy was fifteen the first time someone handed him a rifle and called it an honor. He did not believe it was an honor. He was old enough to recognize the difference between an honor and an obligation dressed in the language of one, but he was also young enough — or perhaps simply frightened enough, which does the same work as youth in most circumstances — to accept it as though he believed the words used to describe it.

Clean it every night, the older cadre told him, without warmth, the way a man might instruct a subordinate in the care of equipment that was never going to belong to him. It is not yours. It belongs to Angkar. The Boy nodded. He understood, without being told, exactly what would happen to him if the rifle went missing, or broke, or simply failed to be maintained to a standard he had not been given and would be judged against regardless. He had learned, in the months since being taken from his village, that most instructions here worked this way — a rule stated plainly, its true stakes left for you to infer, the inference itself functioning as a second, unstated form of control.

At the checkpoint, they put a rifle in his hands and pointed him toward a thin man in spectacles who had done — the Boy was never told what, and had stopped expecting to be told anything by that point — something that made his continued existence a problem for someone else to solve, and had elected the Boy to solve it. His hands shook. He did not let them see the shaking, or thought he didn't; he would never be certain, afterward, whether anyone had noticed and simply not remarked on it, one more unspoken rule in a system built almost entirely out of unspoken rules.

He would not, later, be able to describe with any precision what happened next, not because his memory failed him but because some part of him had made a decision, in the instant itself, not to store it the way memories are usually stored — not as a scene with a beginning and an end, but as a single object, sealed, set aside from the rest of what he allowed himself to think about. He cleaned the rifle that night by firelight for far longer than it needed cleaning. His hands, by then, had stopped shaking. This frightened him more than the shaking had.

◆ ◆ ◆

A rival cadre watched him from across the barracks with an expression the Boy couldn't read and didn't try to. He had learned that trying to read expressions here was its own kind of danger — better to assume the worst reading was accurate and act accordingly, better to trust nothing that presented itself as trustworthy.

He was right not to trust it. Weeks later, he was walked into a clearing by two men he didn't know, the rival cadre trailing a few paces behind with the particular blankness of a man who has already decided something and is only waiting for the formality of it to catch up. No trial. No accusation spoken aloud that the Boy could have answered, even if answers had mattered here, which they did not. He knelt because kneeling was what was expected, and in his final seconds he felt something closer to fatigue than fear — the exhaustion of a boy who had spent longer than he could measure anymore bracing for exactly this, so that its actual arrival came almost as a relief from the bracing itself.

The rival cadre picked the rifle up off the ground afterward, wiped it against his sleeve in a gesture so unceremonious it might have been mistaken, by anyone who hadn't just watched what preceded it, for simple housekeeping. He did not look back. There was, in the logic of the place he had been made by, nothing behind him worth looking at.

The number climbed. It always climbed. No one in the clearing that day would ever see the figure it climbed to, or know they had contributed a single, uncounted digit to it.
