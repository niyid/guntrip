Part Five — Libya, 2011

The Young Rebel had inherited the rifle four days after his older brother died holding it, in an exchange that involved no words because there had been no time for words, only the brother's hand closing around his and pressing the stock into it before the brother went still in a way that the Young Rebel would spend the rest of his short life trying, and failing, to stop seeing whenever he closed his eyes for longer than a blink.

Street fighting in Misrata did not resolve, for him, into anything as coherent as a battle. It came instead as a series of unconnected fragments — a burnt car's heat against his back, an older fighter's voice shouting directions he only half-processed, smoke thick enough to taste. He fired when the men around him fired and could not, afterward, have said with any confidence what any individual shot had accomplished, or cost, or meant.

That night, in the quiet of a borrowed safehouse, he studied a strip of photographs scorched at one corner — his brother visible in one of them, laughing at something outside the frame, in a life that had ended days before but that the photograph insisted, with the particular cruelty of photographs, was still somehow ongoing. He did not cry. He had discovered, in the days since, that crying required a kind of stillness he no longer had access to.

◆ ◆ ◆

Sent out on patrol some streets from where he'd been sleeping, he watched the sky behind him ignite — not distant enough for comfort, not close enough to be certain, until the certainty arrived a half-second later in the form of a sound that reorganized every plan he'd had for the rest of the night into a single directive.

He ran toward it.

What he found, when he arrived, was not a scene that resolved into anything he could describe afterward in a sentence with a clear subject and object. It was rubble, settling in the particular slow way that rubble settles, dust still hanging in what light there was, small fires marking the edges of what had, an hour before, been someone's understanding of where home was. He began searching with his hands because his hands were the only tool available to him and because stopping to look for a better one felt, in that moment, like its own kind of betrayal. Other figures emerged from neighboring buildings to help, silhouettes cut out by flashlight beams, indistinguishable from him and from each other — a temporary, wordless coalition of people doing the only thing any of them could do, which was to keep turning over broken concrete in case turning over the next piece was the one that mattered.

What he found there — or did not find, or found and could never afterward bring himself to describe even to the people closest to him in the years that followed, for those who survived long enough to have years that followed — is not something this account will resolve for you. Some things do not belong to the reader. They belong only to the person who found them, if anyone was found at all, and to the silence that came after.

◆ ◆ ◆

Weeks later, hollowed but still moving through the motions his body had apparently decided to keep performing on his behalf, he gave water from his own canteen to a captured loyalist soldier sitting zip-tied in the dirt, and walked away before the man could find whatever words he might have offered in return. It was not forgiveness. The Young Rebel would not have known how to describe what it was. Perhaps it was only the recognition, arrived at without his permission, that the soldier's thirst and his own exhaustion belonged to the same category of thing the war had made of both of them.

He did not survive the position being overrun weeks after that — a skirmish the account of his life would only ever be able to render in its tail end, dust and distant pops, his body one among several near a wrecked pickup. A smuggler picking through the wreckage afterward handled the rifle the way he handled everything he found there: as inventory, checking its action with practiced hands before adding it to a bundle already strapped to a waiting mule, headed south, toward a market that had never once, in the rifle's decades of movement, run short of buyers.

The searching, unlike the number, resolved into nothing. That was the point the ledger could never capture, no matter how faithfully it counted: some costs do not convert into a figure. They only convert into the particular, permanent weight of having looked, and having had to stop looking, and never fully knowing why you stopped.
