Part One — Vietnam, 1965–1968

The Militiaman had been a farmer for longer than he had been anything else, and he intended, in the private ledger he kept of what he still believed about his own life, to go back to being only that once this was over. He did not examine too closely what "over" meant, or when it might arrive, because both questions belonged to a category of thinking that got men killed faster than any ambush did — the thinking that assumed there was a clean seam between the war and the life on either side of it, when in fact the war had long since stopped being a season and become the weather.

The rifle came to him at dawn, in mist thick enough that the cadre officer walking the line seemed to materialize rather than approach. It was handed to him the way a tool is handed to a man already expected to know its use — no ceremony, no instruction. He turned it once in his hands, felt the mismatched stock under his palm, cool and slightly rougher on one side than the other where an old repair hadn't quite matched grain to grain, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the weight of it hadn't already said for him.

That night he leaned it in the corner of his one-room house the way he leaned the hoe, the coil of rope — an object with a job, no more sacred than any other. His wife ladled thin porridge into two bowls without looking at it, though he noticed her not-looking, which was its own kind of looking. Their son slept in the hammock behind them, six years old and unbothered by the artillery grumbling somewhere over the tree line, because he had never known a version of the night that didn't grumble.

They ate in silence. It was not an unhappy silence. It was the silence of two people conserving a resource neither of them could have named, though if pressed, the Militiaman might have called it hope, and might have been embarrassed to call it that, because hope had come to feel like a luxury item best kept out of sight, the way you didn't wear your good clothes into a rice paddy.

The tunnel came later — a different kind of dark, close and packed with the smell of wet earth and cordite. He shared a cigarette with a young American whose wrists were bound and whose eyes, in the brief flare of the match, held an expression the Militiaman recognized without wanting to: not fear exactly, but the particular exhaustion of a man doing arithmetic on how many more days he had left, and coming up, each time, with a number he didn't trust. The Militiaman held the cigarette to the boy's lips because the boy's hands couldn't reach it themselves, and neither of them said anything, because there was, again, nothing that needed saying. Outside, rain began finding its way into the tunnel mouth, one drop landing in the same low spot again and again, and both men watched it fall for far longer than made any tactical sense, because watching it fall was, for those minutes, the only thing either of them was required to do.

◆ ◆ ◆

Weeks passed the way weeks pass when a person has stopped counting them for any purpose beyond survival. There was a morning — ordinary, unremarkable, the kind of morning that later becomes unbearable to remember precisely because nothing in it warned anyone — when the Militiaman worked the paddy beside his wife, rifle slung across his back the way another man might carry a hoe over his shoulder, its threat entirely absent in the daylight, folded away like a knife put back in a drawer. Their son chased a duck through the shallow water, laughing at nothing, at everything, at the simple fact of being six and having a duck to chase.

For perhaps thirty seconds, this could have been any year. It could have been no war at all.

Then the sound arrived — not sudden, but rising, the particular whistle of something enormous and very far above them, already too late to matter by the time it became audible at all. He looked up. He understood, in the half-second available to him for understanding anything, that there was no version of the next several seconds in which running mattered, and he ran anyway, because a man's legs do not consult his understanding before they move toward his family.

The strike arrived as light before it arrived as sound — a wall of white that swallowed the paddy, the duck, the laughter, everything.

When the light receded into an ordinary, terrible quiet, the rifle lay half-submerged in the churned water, its stock the one clean surface in a frame otherwise given over entirely to mud and stillness. Nothing else in the frame moved. It would be picked up, eventually, by someone else's hands, on someone else's ordinary morning.

Somewhere, in a ledger no one living was keeping and everyone dead had contributed to, a number that had stood at zero for twelve years began, quietly, to climb. It would not ask permission to keep climbing. It never had.
